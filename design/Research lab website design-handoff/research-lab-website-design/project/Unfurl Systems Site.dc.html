Core question

If AI is going to govern an interconnected world, where is the universal harness that tells it what everything is, what it can do, what it depends on, and what rules it must obey?

Every major computing shift has created a new harness: a layer that makes raw capability understandable, usable, and governable by its primary consumer. The command line exposed computation to technical users. The graphical user interface made computing legible to everyday humans. APIs, SDKs, cloud platforms, and orchestration systems made software capabilities callable by other software.

AI is now becoming a new kind of consumer. It does not merely click buttons or call APIs. Increasingly, it is expected to reason across tools, devices, services, environments, and decisions. Yet the harness we give AI today is still fragmented. We describe tools. We expose endpoints. We register actions. But we rarely provide a universal, domain-aware contract that tells AI what an entity is, what it can do, what it requires, what it affects, and what governs its use.

Every era had a harness

A harness is an enabling layer. It takes something powerful but difficult to use and makes it accessible to its intended consumer. The GUI was not just decoration: it translated machine capability into a form humans could understand, providing affordances, constraints, feedback, and a mental model. In that sense it was not merely a user interface. It was a capability harness for human cognition.

Now AI is creating the next shift. The primary consumer is no longer only a human, and not only another deterministic software program. The consumer is an intelligent agent expected to reason, choose, compose, and act. That agent needs a new kind of harness.

The connected world does not fail because a device lacks an API. It fails when intelligence cannot understand what the device is, what it means, and what rules govern its use.

The air-conditioner test

Consider a simple air-conditioner. An AI system may be able to turn it on, set a temperature, or change a mode. That is useful, but it is not enough for intelligent governance. To govern even this ordinary device well, AI may need a richer self-description.

Question AI must answerExample answer from the entity itself
What domain does this entity serve?HVAC / climate control
What capabilities does it offer?Cool, heat, dehumidify, fan, sleep mode
What are its operating constraints?1.5 ton, specific voltage range, safe temperature limits
What does it depend on?Power supply, thermostat, network connectivity, room sensor
What policies govern it?Energy limits, occupancy rules, user permissions, safety limits
What relationships does it have?Serves Room 204, belongs to Floor 2, part of Building A climate system

If this information is unavailable or inconsistent, AI must infer. Inference may work in a demo. It is not enough for a world where AI is expected to operate across real systems, real constraints, and real consequences.

Toward Domain Claim Protocol

A Domain Claim Protocol can be thought of as a structured claim that an entity makes about itself: its domain, capabilities, requirements, constraints, policies, relationships, and activation rules. In that framing, DCP becomes a candidate for the missing harness: a capability contract that AI can read before it acts.

The important point is not to start with the protocol. The important point is to recognize the architectural gap. The GUI helped humans use computers because it translated machine capability into human-understandable form. The next harness must help AI govern the connected world by translating domain capability into AI-understandable form.

There is a strange anxiety running through the software industry right now. It shows up in different words — productivity, automation, agents, copilots, replacement — but underneath, the question is almost always the same: if AI can write more and more code, what happens to software development?

The deeper shift is not that software developers are becoming unnecessary. The deeper shift is that the baseline of software work is moving. Higher-level languages lifted us above raw instructions. Operating systems lifted us above hardware. GUIs lifted ordinary users above command lines. Cloud platforms lifted teams above physical infrastructure. Now AI is pushing the baseline again.

Connected is not the same as composable

A device can be online and still be opaque. It can be controllable and still be semantically weak. It can expose commands and still fail to explain its domain. An AI trying to manage a building needs more than commands: it needs capacity, energy characteristics, supported modes, operational constraints, sensor dependencies, maintenance state, safety boundaries, cost implications, and the relationship to occupancy, access control, energy pricing, and building policy.

The new unit of software value

The new unit of value is not just a component. It is a governable component. A governable component is not merely callable. It is understandable. It declares its boundaries instead of letting integrators discover them through production incidents. It identifies dependencies before deployment, not after failure. It makes conflict visible when two systems claim authority over the same decision. It treats faults as part of the contract rather than as vague log noise.

The developer of the next era will not be valued only for typing implementation faster than a model. They will be valued for shaping systems that models can safely assemble.

Intelligence during assembly, determinism during execution

Tool access answers only one class of question: how can I invoke this? The bigger connected-world question is different. Should this be composed here at all? What does this component claim to own? Which policy applies? Which component has authority when two claims overlap? Those are not runtime invocation questions. They are assembly questions.

An interconnected world needs a design-time composition layer, where intelligence can reason slowly, structurally, and safely before runtime. Once composition is settled, runtime should be fast, deterministic, and governed by frozen decisions — not reinterpreted on every call by a model.

That shift is why the first essay asked for a missing harness. The missing harness is not simply about connecting tools. It is about giving every meaningful component a way to describe itself as part of a larger composition. That is where Part 3 begins.

Part 1 named the missing harness: AI cannot safely govern a connected world if every component describes itself in a different way, or worse, does not describe itself at all. Part 2 reframed the anxiety inside software development: the baseline is shifting from writing isolated components to assembling, governing, validating, and operating systems of capabilities.

This third essay gives that missing harness a name: DCP, the Domain Claim Protocol. DCP is the idea that every meaningful component in an AI-native world should carry a self-description that is useful not only to humans, but also to agents. Not a brochure. Not a README. Not a loose API document. A structured claim about what the component is, what it owns, what it refuses, what it needs, what it offers, and how it can safely compose with other components.

Core idea

The future does not need every object to become a chatbot. It needs every capable object to explain itself well enough that agents can compose it safely.

The most important part: what it refuses

The surprisingly powerful part of DCP is not only what a component claims to do. It is what it explicitly refuses to own. Refusal is how a component becomes trustworthy. A component that says “I own identity verification, but I do not own authorization decisions; those belong to a policy engine” gives the system a boundary it can reason about.

A component that says “I can do everything” is not powerful; it is dangerous.

Most failures in large systems are not caused by lack of capability. They are caused by confused ownership. Who owns the decision? Who owns the state? Who owns the failure? Who is allowed to remediate it? DCP makes those questions first-class. Agents are very good at stitching fragments together; without boundaries, that stitching becomes improvisation. DCP is a way to make the stitching accountable.

The three planes of DCP

Once components have claims, DCP does not jump directly into execution. It introduces negotiation: taking two claims, plus context, and deciding whether and how they compose. The output of negotiation is not a prompt. It is not an opinion. It is a frozen composition contract.

This separation prevents a common AI architecture mistake: putting the reasoning layer into the path of every request. If every operation requires an agent to rethink the contract, the system becomes slow, hard to audit, and difficult to deploy inside customer boundaries.

A concrete example: the smart room

An AI agent can turn on the AC. That is useful, but it is not the big picture. The bigger question is: should it turn on the AC? At what temperature? For which occupant? Under which energy policy? What if the curtain system can reduce heat load more efficiently? What if access control says the person is a visitor and building policy forbids changing room settings?

In a DCP-style world, the AC does not merely say “set_temperature is available.” It says: I own thermal comfort execution for this room, I can cool or heat within these limits, I depend on electricity and a room sensor, I refuse access-control decisions, I refuse energy-budget authority, and I emit these faults when compressor, sensor, or policy constraints block operation. Now the agent is no longer guessing. It is composing against declared claims.

DCP, APIs, and MCP: different altitudes

An API answers: how can software call this capability? MCP answers: how can an LLM invoke this tool at runtime? DCP answers a different question: should this component compose with that component, under what ownership boundaries, with which dependency satisfaction, through which contract, and with what runtime binding? MCP is about invocation in the moment. DCP is about assembly before the moment.

Old baselineAI-native baseline
Write the componentDefine the component and its claim
Document the APIDeclare domain, offers, dependencies, and refusals
Manually wire integrationsValidate and bind capabilities through contracts
Debug failures from logsPropagate typed faults with declared blast radius
Runtime improvisation by agentsDesign-time negotiation, runtime execution

The most important result is not automation. The most important result is accountability. When something composes, we can ask why. When it refuses, we can ask what should own the concern instead. When it fails, we can trace what was affected. In that sense, DCP is the missing harness made concrete: a capability contract for an AI-native world.

Discussion draft posture

This part intentionally starts with the current draft shape of DCP. The explanation and the invitation to contribute come after the reader has seen what is being proposed.

In the earlier parts of this series, we moved from the missing harness to the software baseline shift, and then to DCP as a capability contract for an AI-native world. Part 4 should do something slightly different. It should not begin by asking people to comment on an abstract idea. It should first show the current draft: the shape of the protocol, the main primitives, the guardrails, and the questions it is trying to answer.

The following is not a final standard. It is a public-facing working shape of the current DCP draft: enough structure to make the proposal concrete, while still leaving room for domain feedback and design correction. DCP is designed for late-bound enterprise composition. Instead of hardcoding every application topology, components publish claims, and those claims are resolved through requirements, policies, context, and bindings.

Core primitives in the draft

PrimitiveWhat it answers
ClaimWhat is this component, what domain does it own, and what does it refuse?
NegotiationCan two claims compose under this context, policy, tenant, and environment?
Composition contractWhat exact need binds to what exact offer, with what transport, mapping, expectations, provenance, trust, and invalidation rules?
Runtime bindingWhere and how is the frozen contract wired in a specific environment or tenant?
Aggregate componentHow can a parent capability contain and govern child components, contracts, and bindings?
Fault vocabularyHow can a component fail, what does the failure affect, and when may it propagate?

Illustrative claim fragment

A claim is the component self-description. It is static, published per component version, and treated as the source of truth for what the component owns and refuses. The full schema is larger, but even a small fragment shows the shape DCP wants to make explicit.

identity:
  uri: dcp://components/smart-room/hvac
  name: Room HVAC Adapter
  kind: component
  version: 1.0.0
domain:
  concerns:
    - concern: room-climate-control
      scope_notes: Owns local temperature execution, not building-wide energy policy.
  boundary_principles:
    - Comfort decisions may be constrained by safety, occupancy, and energy policy.
refusals:
  - concern: building-wide-energy-policy
    owned_by: energy-optimization-system
offers:
  - capability: room.climate.setpoint
dependencies:
  required:
    - need: occupancy-signal
faults:
  emitted:
    - code: hvac.sensor_stale
      severity: degraded

Claim sections in the draft: identity, domain, refusals, dependencies, offers, conflict_resolution, negotiation_surface, integration_ports, faults, and metadata.

Aggregation is not an optional extension

Aggregation is how DCP represents a larger capability made from smaller capabilities. A smart room, workflow, module, product surface, or enterprise domain can behave as an aggregate component while still preserving the claims, contracts, and runtime bindings of its children. In the draft shape it appears at three levels: a parent claim references child claims, a parent contract references child contracts, and a parent runtime binding references child bindings.

Public draft note

If aggregation is missing, the spec collapses back into pairwise integration. For an interconnected world, the parent/child composition graph must be part of the protocol surface itself.

Now open it for discussion

Once the draft shape is visible, the invitation becomes more precise. The question is no longer, “Do you like DCP?” The better question is: can this draft describe the components, devices, services, workflows, and policies from your domain without lying, oversimplifying, or hiding risk?

Questions for readers:

  • Can this draft describe physical-world devices without pretending they are ordinary APIs?
  • Can it describe aggregates such as smart rooms, workflows, product modules, or enterprise domains without hiding child components?
  • Are the boundary and refusal concepts strong enough for regulated domains?
  • Does the fault vocabulary capture the failures that operators actually care about?
  • Where should the draft force human escalation rather than allow automated negotiation?
  • What should be standardized, and what should remain policy-specific?

The most valuable contribution is a concrete case. Pick one component from your world and try to describe it through the draft spec. Then identify where the draft works, where it feels awkward, and where it fails.

In the earlier parts of this series, we started with a missing layer: a harness that allows AI to understand what components are, what they can do, what they refuse to do, and how they can be safely composed. Part 4 moved that idea closer to a draft protocol.

Part 5 completes another side of the story. If DCP is the AI harness, then there must also be a human harness. Not because humans need to micromanage every AI decision, but because every meaningful system still needs human review, human intent, human accountability, and human imagination. The human harness is not merely a dashboard. It is the surface through which people can inspect, manipulate, validate, and govern AI-native assemblies without losing the underlying truth.

The clue was already in the schema

Some fields in the DCP-facing material do not look like they are meant only for AI. Shape, fallback form, visual category, port anchors, connector shapes, interaction handles: these are not fields an AI needs in order to reason about capability compatibility. They are fields a human needs in order to see capability compatibility.

A component should carry one source of truth, with multiple projections. One projection makes it legible to AI. Another makes it legible to humans. This is why the human harness should not be bolted on after the protocol is finished. It is already implied by the protocol.

Unfurl Studio: the human harness

Unfurl Studio is the design-time surface where operators can compose intelligent components visually, inspect the DCP facts behind them, and export the same signed contract that the non-visual pipeline would produce. It is one product expression of this pattern, not a requirement of DCP itself: another authoring surface can project the same claims, contracts, runtime bindings, faults, and review questions if it preserves DCP as the source of truth.

The important part is what Studio is not. It is not a free-form diagramming canvas. It is not a place where moving shapes magically creates a contract. It is not a shortcut around DCP, Fabric, trust policy, or compatibility validation. The human moves shapes, connects ports, or reviews a suggested change. But Fabric validates the intent, DCP decides compatibility, and the signed contract comes from the compiler.

The AI should propose; the human should dispose; Fabric should validate.

Visual does not mean vague

Many technical people distrust visual tools because visual tools often become informal. Boxes and arrows drift away from what actually runs. The human harness has to avoid that failure. Every handle maps to a declared capability, dependency, substrate dependency, or refusal. Every semantic action becomes an intent. Every export comes from the compiler.

That is the difference between a diagramming surface and a governed composition surface. A diagram lets a human describe what they hope is true. A human harness lets a human manipulate what the system knows is true. Part 6 explains why that harness must also support aggregation and semantic zoom.

In Part 5, we introduced the human harness: the visual and interactive layer that lets people inspect, review, and govern what AI and DCP assemble. That harness only works if it can show the right level of detail at the right time. A user should not be forced to inspect every sensor, tap, geyser, AC mode, identity provider, storage connector, and runtime binding just to understand whether a house, colony, workflow, or city is healthy enough to reason about.

Part 6 argues that aggregation is the scaling principle. An aggregate is not merely a bag of child components. A house is not just AC plus geyser plus taps plus doors plus curtains. At the level of the house, it exposes higher-level capabilities: comfort, security, energy posture, water readiness, automation level. The underlying components remain there, but they are not the first thing every agent or human needs to see.

Aggregation is not the act of piling capabilities together. It is the act of giving a collection a new, meaningful surface.

The map analogy

A map does not show every house, window, tap, switch, cable, and pipe when you are looking at a city. At the city level, it shows city-level meaning. At the street level, it shows streets, buildings, entrances, and traffic. The details have not disappeared. They are simply not projected at the wrong level.

AI-native systems need the same discipline. A city-level agent should not reason directly over every geyser in every house. It should reason over abstractions like water demand, energy flexibility, automation coverage, infrastructure risk, and service availability. Showing everything at once is not transparency. It is noise with a nicer font.

Containment is different from compatibility

A child is inside an aggregate because the aggregate contains it. A replacement candidate is compatible because it can satisfy a role. Those are different relationships, and they should not be blurred. A colony contains houses. That does not mean every house is a replacement for every other house. The containment graph tells us what exists inside. The compatibility graph tells us what can replace or connect.

The same idea continues after design-time description. A parent contract can reference child contracts; each child remains a normal contract with its own parties, binding, transport, expectations, provenance, trust, and invalidation rules. Runtime bindings follow the same principle, which gives us a runtime structure that can be observed, audited, explained, and regenerated without inventing a parallel wiring format.

Without aggregation, intelligence drowns in detail. With aggregation, intelligence can reason at the right scale. Part 7 turns to the next problem: runtime life.

Part 6 introduced aggregation as abstraction. Part 7 moves from design-time assembly to runtime life. Once an assembly is created, loaded, and bound to a real environment, the human harness must remain useful. It should not become a pretty design tool that disappears when the system starts running. It should become the operator surface for loading, monitoring, stress testing, tracing, auditing, and evolving the assembly.

The key idea is simple: monitoring should be projected onto the assembly humans understand. Logs, metrics, traces, and dashboards are necessary, but they are not enough. A DCP-grounded system knows the claims, contracts, runtime bindings, faults, dependencies, and aggregation levels that give those signals meaning.

Loading an assembly is contract hydration

A traditional deployment asks a narrow question: can we put this software somewhere and start it? An AI-native assembly asks a richer question: can we load this signed composition into a real environment without losing the meaning of the contract? Loading should mean projecting a frozen design-time agreement into a live environment while preserving the semantic relationship between each component and the resources it uses.

Runtime layerQuestion it answers
Composition contractWhat is valid?

Faults as semantic events

A failure is not automatically a fault. A log line might say that an API timed out. DCP asks a more structured question: is this a declared fault? What capability does it affect? Does it impact a parent claim? What evidence justifies it? What remediation is allowed?

That distinction matters because runtime systems need restraint. A child component failure should not automatically become a city-level incident. A temporary sensor blip should not automatically disable a house. A refused action should not be treated as a bug if the component explicitly refuses ownership of that concern. In a DCP-shaped runtime view, faults carry a code, category, severity, affected needs, affected offers, evidence references, correlation identifiers, and propagation rules.

The accelerator pedal for systems

Runtime observation is not only about waiting for failures. The operator should be able to select stress parameters and watch how the assembly behaves. Increase users. Increase sessions. Increase model calls. Tighten policies. Add regional constraints. Then watch which capabilities degrade first, which faults propagate, which contracts hold, and which assumptions break.

The result is not just a graph of CPU and memory. The result is an operational story: at this load, this capability degrades; this dependency saturates; this fault propagates to the parent; this remediation remains allowed; this human approval is required before the system can proceed.

A system is not complete when it is described, assembled, or even deployed. It becomes a usable intelligent system when humans can understand it while it runs.

A living assembly must be allowed to evolve, but evolution must not become silent mutation. If a component is replaced, the replacement should still satisfy the parent need. If a claim version changes, the relevant contract should be reviewed or renegotiated. If a human overrides the default path, the decision should be recorded. That is the line between safe evolution and hidden drift.

Domain Claim Protocol

A way for a component to state what it can do, what it needs, and under what conditions it may be invoked — written down so an assembly can be checked rather than trusted.

View the repository ↗ Read the draft spec

The problem

Components describe their interface but not their behaviour, cost, or boundaries. A human or a model assembling several of them has to guess at what happens under conditions the interface never mentions — a missing input, a region the data cannot legally leave, a latency budget the caller actually needs. DCP is an attempt to make those guesses unnecessary by giving components a shared way to write them down.

How it works

A

Claim

B

Registry

C

Match

D

Contract

↓ consumed by

Fabric (compiler) · Studio (review)

A component publishes a claim into a registry. When something needs a capability, Fabric matches the need against candidate claims and produces a contract — the negotiated, signed record of what will actually be provided. Every later invocation can be checked against that contract rather than against the original, looser claim.

Text alternative to the diagram: a claim is published to a registry, matched against a need to produce a contract, and the contract is what Fabric and Studio consume.

Key concepts

Claim

What a component provides, what it requires, and the bounds it operates under.

Bounds

Constraints such as data residency or latency that a match must respect. Still the least settled part of the schema.

Contract

The signed, negotiated outcome of matching a need against a claim.

Full schema reference lives in the repository README and /spec.

About

A research posture, not a product roadmap.

Unfurl Systems studies how software components can describe themselves, compose under contract, and stay legible to the people accountable for them. We publish the reasoning as we go, alongside the specifications and prototypes that reasoning produces.

Research-first, not launch-first

We would rather publish an argument with visible gaps than a polished claim we can't yet back up. Nothing on this site should be read as an announcement of a finished product. Specifications are drafts until they say otherwise; prototypes are prototypes.

Published specifications and prototypes on this site are not necessarily production-ready, and we say so on every page where it matters.

Principles

Status is not optional

Every idea, spec, and prototype carries a maturity label, on the site and in its repository.

Summarize, don't duplicate

The website explains; the repository is the source of truth for what actually runs.

Disagreement is useful input

A well-argued objection or a domain counterexample moves the work forward faster than agreement does.

Publish only what's ready to be read

We would rather ship four public Labs with clear boundaries than promote every internal experiment.

Spacing & structure

8px base unit. Section vertical padding 44–64px. Card padding 18–28px. Content max-width 1160px for grids, 700px for reading. 1px hairline borders (#e5e2da) separate sections instead of shadows; cards use a 1px border and 3px radius, never a drop shadow or gradient.

Mobile behavior notes

  • Header nav wraps to a second row under ~560px rather than collapsing to a hamburger; four labels plus the GitHub pill fit two lines comfortably and stay keyboard/tab visible.
  • All grids are repeat(auto-fit,minmax(...)), so Lab/article/pillar cards reflow to one column with no separate mobile layout to maintain.
  • Reading measure caps at 700px and never needs adjustment on mobile; padding drops from 24px to 16–20px at the section level only.
  • Diagram figures (contract lifecycle, DCP flow) stack their steps into a single column under ~480px; the plain-text alternative under each figure is always present, not mobile-only.
  • Status pills and metadata rows wrap via flex-wrap rather than truncating — status must never be hidden behind a "more" affordance.
  • Sticky header stays but drops box-shadow-like elevation in favor of the hairline border, keeping it visually quiet on small screens.
  • Tap targets (nav items, card buttons, footer links) hold a minimum 44px effective height via padding even where the visible label is small.

Components shown across the site

Status strip, one-sentence definition, problem, architecture diagram + text alternative, scope table (implemented/specified/experimental/deferred), key concepts, examples, related research links, repository callout, participate block — see the DCP Lab page for the full pattern in context.
